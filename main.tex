\begin{abstract}
Jigsaw puzzle reassembly remains a highly challenging task, particularly when piece boundaries are irregularly eroded to simulate damage to cultural artifacts. On the GAP benchmark, existing methods achieve a perfect assembly accuracy of only 0.3\% on $5\times5$ puzzles, whereas humans can solve jigsaw puzzles with remarkably high accuracy. Humans typically approach puzzle solving by identifying potentially adjacent pieces, evaluating the current assembly, and iteratively performing complex operations such as adding pieces, replacing incorrectly placed pieces, and moving entire connected groups of pieces. However, existing methods face a fundamental limitation: approaches that leverage global visual information typically predict piece arrangements directly, making it difficult to explicitly perform such complex iterative operations, while search based methods can rearrange pieces but primarily rely on pairwise boundary compatibility, lacking global visual information to evaluate and guide the search process.
To bridge this gap, we propose GlobalPuzzle, a jigsaw puzzle reassembly framework that integrates global canvas level evaluation, action guidance, and genetic search. By incorporating global visual information into the evaluation and optimization of candidate assemblies, GlobalPuzzle aims to resolve ambiguous adjacency relationships that are difficult to determine from irregularly eroded piece boundaries alone, enabling flexible, globally informed reconstruction.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Jigsaw puzzle reassembly with missing visual information is challenging due to the enormous search space and irregularly eroded piece boundaries~\cite{shahar2026gap}.
An $N \times N$ puzzle has $(N^2)!$ possible arrangements, making the probability of randomly solving a $5 \times 5$ puzzle only $1/25! \approx 6.44695 \times 10^{-26}$.
Boundary erosion disrupts visual continuity, while smaller pieces provide less information for determining their positions and adjacency relationships~\cite{bridger2020eroded,paumard2020deepzzle}.
Even advanced AI agents such as Claude Sonnet 5.5 Medium and Codex require approximately 10 minutes on average to solve a $3 \times 3$ GAP puzzle and rarely reconstruct $5 \times 5$ puzzles correctly.

Existing methods face two major limitations. On the one hand, many deep learning approaches exchange information among fragments through attention mechanisms and directly predict or iteratively refine their arrangements~\cite{scarpellini2024diffassemble,liu2024jpdvt,kim2025fcvit,shahar2026gap}. However, such feature interaction does not itself provide a visual fitness score for the spatially assembled image. These methods also lack explicit operations for preserving established fragment connections and moving connected groups as a whole. On the other hand, combinatorial optimization methods can explore different arrangements and preserve fragment relationships through genetic operations~\cite{pomeranz2011greedy,sholomon2013genetic}, but typically evaluate candidates by aggregating pairwise compatibility scores. When fragment boundaries are severely eroded, local boundary evidence may be insufficient to determine whether an arrangement forms a globally coherent image. Learned tree search can evaluate partial assemblies~\cite{paumard2023alphazzle}, but its piece placement actions do not explicitly relocate connected groups.

Preserving connected fragment groups is an established principle of traditional genetic puzzle solvers~\cite{sholomon2013genetic}. Their crossover operators inherit shared connections and relocate assembled segments while preserving internal relationships. GlobalPuzzle builds on this existing principle: connected group preservation itself is not our contribution. Mutation and population diversity allow inherited structures to be revised, but effective search also needs reliable placement guidance and candidate evaluation. Our focus is to supply both decisions with global visual information from the assembled canvas.

Motivated by these observations, we propose GlobalPuzzle, which couples explicit fragment group operations with visual guidance from assembled canvases. The Policy Network uses the partial canvas to guide placement, while the Evaluation Network scores complete canvases to guide genetic search. Crossover preserves and relocates shared connections, and mutation allows incorrect structures to be revised. Figure~\ref{fig:agent_comparison} illustrates the reconstruction task and position querying. Grid pooling and position to fragment matching allow the same trained model to process different canvas sizes, fragment sizes, and grid dimensions. Our contributions are threefold:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We integrate the established connected group inheritance of genetic puzzle solvers with learned global canvas evaluation and placement guidance in a unified reassembly framework.
    \item We develop a model that accommodates different image dimensions, fragment sizes, and rectangular grids without retraining, enabling reuse beyond the puzzle configurations used for training.
    \item We demonstrate improved reconstruction of severely eroded puzzles over the evaluated baselines, reaching perfect assembly accuracies of $69.2\%$ and $19.5\%$ on GAP-3 and GAP-5. Controlled ablations establish the contributions of global evaluation, placement guidance, and iterative search.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Direct Arrangement Prediction Methods}

Learning based solvers predict permutations, coordinates, or sequences from fragment appearance. JigsawGAN~\cite{li2022jigsawgan} couples permutation classification with an image generation branch through feature warping. Its adversarial objective supplies image semantic supervision during training. FCViT~\cite{kim2025fcvit} instead uses a Vision Transformer~\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit} to regress each fragment's horizontal and vertical coordinates, avoiding classification over a predefined permutation set. PuzLM~\cite{elkin2026puzlm} quantizes pieces into discrete token sequences and uses a sequence to sequence language model to generate the reconstructed order.

Other methods refine arrangements through successive predictions. DiffAssemble~\cite{scarpellini2024diffassemble} represents fragments as graph nodes and learns to denoise their translations and rotations. JPDVT~\cite{liu2024jpdvt} uses diffusion Transformers to recover positional encodings conditioned on visual content, including settings with missing pieces. PuzzleFlow~\cite{shahar2026gap} combines Vision Transformer features with discrete flow matching to update fragment positions conditioned on the current arrangement and flow time.

These approaches use fragment interactions or image semantic supervision to reason beyond individual boundaries. However, training an image generator or exchanging fragment features does not supply a learned fitness function that evaluates complete candidate canvases during search. Furthermore, iterative position updates do not explicitly identify connected groups, preserve their internal relationships, and relocate them as units. We combine this structural operation with global visual evaluation and placement guidance.

\subsection{Compatibility Based and Learned Search}

Compatibility based solvers separate adjacency estimation from arrangement optimization. Cho et al.~\cite{cho2010probabilistic} formulate assembly as graphical model inference with pairwise compatibility and location evidence. Pomeranz et al.~\cite{pomeranz2011greedy} combine boundary compatibility with greedy placement and segment rearrangement. Gallagher~\cite{gallagher2012orientation} uses gradient compatibility and constrained tree assembly to handle unknown piece orientations. Paikin and Tal~\cite{paikin2015missing} extend greedy assembly to mixed puzzles with missing pieces. Sholomon et al.~\cite{sholomon2013genetic} use crossover to inherit shared segments from parent solutions, preserving connections while changing their locations. Yu et al.~\cite{yu2016linear} jointly optimize positions through successive linear programs with a weighted $\ell_1$ objective. These methods support structural search, but their objectives aggregate pairwise scores.

Learned matching addresses the loss of boundary continuity. Deepzzle~\cite{paumard2020deepzzle} predicts relative fragment positions and uses shortest path optimization to select an assembly, including puzzles with missing or extraneous fragments. Bridger et al.~\cite{bridger2020eroded} inpaint gaps between candidate neighbors and reuse the discriminator to classify adjacency. Both improve placement evidence while retaining local fragment comparisons as the basis for assembly.

Alphazzle~\cite{paumard2023alphazzle} instead guides Monte Carlo tree search with policy and value networks that process the current reassembly. Its actions place the next fragment into an empty position. This provides visual guidance for sequential construction, but does not explicitly move an already connected group as a unit. GlobalPuzzle combines complete canvas evaluation with structural inheritance, connected group relocation, and placement guidance, allowing established arrangements to be revised during search.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Framework Overview}
\label{sec:overview}

Figure~\ref{fig:all} shows the framework. Given $M=N^2$ unordered image fragments $\mathcal{P}=\{I_i\}_{i=1}^{M}$, we seek a permutation $\pi$ assigning one fragment to each position of an $N\times N$ grid. Let $C(\pi)$ denote the resulting RGB canvas. GlobalPuzzle searches for the assembly maximizing a learned quality function:
\begin{equation}
    \hat\pi=\arg\max_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_M}F_\theta(\pi),\qquad
    F_\theta(\pi)=\sigma\bigl(A_\theta(C(\pi))\bigr).
    \label{eq:gp-objective}
\end{equation}
The framework combines a global evaluation network $A_\theta$, a canvas conditioned placement policy $B_\phi$, and genetic search. The policy proposes placements, genetic operations preserve and rearrange fragment groups, and the evaluator selects promising complete assemblies.

Before assembly, we independently apply LaMa~\cite{suvorov2022lama} to each eroded puzzle piece without access to the ground-truth arrangement or complete image. This provides dense RGB inputs for subsequent processing but does not guarantee pixel or boundary continuity between adjacent pieces.
\subsection{Evaluation Network}
\label{sec:global-eval}

\begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{pic/ABC.pdf}
    \caption{
       The specific structure of the Evaluation Network and Policy Network.
    }
    \label{fig:abc}
\end{figure*}

\par\noindent{\textbf{Architecture.}}
The evaluator in Figure~\ref{fig:abc} processes the complete canvas using a multiscale CNN~\cite{woo2023convnextv2} followed by a Transformer~\cite{vaswani2017attention}. Features pooled to the puzzle grid retain spatial organization, while a global token and spatial readout provide complementary representations. The network outputs a quality logit $z_\theta=A_\theta(C(\pi))$ and a fitness score $\hat Q(\pi)=\sigma(z_\theta)$. An auxiliary head estimates absolute accuracy during training.
\par\noindent{\textbf{Quality Supervision.}}
Let $\pi^*$ be the ground truth, $r_\pi(i)$ the grid coordinate of fragment $i$, and $\mathcal E^*$ the set of $2N(N-1)$ oriented ground truth adjacencies $(i,j,\delta)$. We define:
\begin{align}
\mathrm{AA}(\pi)&=\frac1M\sum_{p=1}^{M}\mathbf1[\pi(p)=\pi^*(p)],\label{eq:gp-aa}\\
\mathrm{SRA}(\pi)&=\frac1{|\mathcal E^*|}\sum_{(i,j,\delta)\in\mathcal E^*}
\mathbf1[r_\pi(j)-r_\pi(i)=\delta],\label{eq:gp-sra}\\
Q(\pi)&=0.6\,\mathrm{AA}(\pi)+0.4\,\mathrm{SRA}(\pi).
\label{eq:gp-quality}
\end{align}
We generate training assemblies with controlled displacement counts and structural transformations, including block exchanges, row and column shifts, and mixed mutations.
\par\noindent{\textbf{Training Objective.}}
The evaluator is trained with logit regression, score calibration, within puzzle ranking, hard negative mining, and auxiliary AA regression:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_A=\mathcal L_{\rm reg}+0.5\mathcal L_{\rm cal}
+0.75\mathcal L_{\rm rank}+0.5\mathcal L_{\rm hard}
+0.2\mathcal L_{\rm AA}.
\label{eq:gp-a-loss}
\end{equation}
Regression targets are $\operatorname{logit}(\operatorname{clip}(Q,0.01,0.99))$. For candidates $i,j$ of the same puzzle with $Q_i>Q_j$, the ranking term penalizes:
\begin{equation}
\ell_{ij}=\operatorname{softplus}\bigl(3(Q_i-Q_j)-z_i+z_j\bigr),
\label{eq:gp-rank}
\end{equation}
with larger weights for greater quality differences and structural negatives. The hard negative term penalizes the most overestimated incorrect candidate relative to the correct assembly.

\subsection{Policy Network}
\label{sec:placement-policy}

The policy in Figure~\ref{fig:abc} encodes all fragments into tokens $c_i$ and extracts a query $q_p$ for each grid position from the partial RGB canvas and its occupancy map. A joint Transformer updates both token sets. The resulting pairwise scores are:
\begin{equation}
S_{p,i}=\tau\,
\frac{\langle W_q\tilde q_p,W_c\tilde c_i\rangle}
{\|W_q\tilde q_p\|_2\,\|W_c\tilde c_i\|_2},
\label{eq:gp-policy}
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is learned. One forward pass produces an $M\times M$ position to fragment score matrix.

Training uses correct and perturbed partial canvases. When all occupied fragments are correctly placed, empty positions receive one hot ground truth targets. Otherwise, occupied neighbors vote for fragments that preserve their ground truth directional relationships. Normalized vote counts define targets $t_{p,i}$, and the policy minimizes:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_B=-\frac1{|\mathcal Q|}\sum_{p\in\mathcal Q}\sum_{i=1}^{M}
 t_{p,i}\log\operatorname{softmax}(S_{p,:})_i,
\label{eq:gp-b-loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal Q$ contains supervised empty positions. During search, occupied positions and used fragments are masked. Fragment embeddings are cached across candidates.

\subsection{Genetic Search with Global Fitness}
\label{sec:ga}
\par\noindent{\textbf{Initialization and Selection.}}
The policy generates an initial population of complete permutations. Each is evaluated by $F_\theta$. The strongest individuals are retained as elites, while parents are sampled with fitness weighted probabilities:
\begin{equation}
P(\pi_k)=\frac{(F_\theta(\pi_k)+\epsilon)^\beta}
{\sum_j(F_\theta(\pi_j)+\epsilon)^\beta}.
\label{eq:gp-selection}
\end{equation}
\par\noindent{\textbf{Structural Crossover.}}
Following the segment inheritance principle of traditional genetic solvers~\cite{sholomon2013genetic}, we preserve parent shared structure. Given parents $\pi^a$ and $\pi^b$, identical absolute placements are copied into the offspring:
\begin{equation}
s_p=\pi^a(p)\quad\text{if }\pi^a(p)=\pi^b(p).
\label{eq:gp-absolute}
\end{equation}
We then identify oriented adjacencies shared by both parents. Their connected components form relative fragment groups. When a fragment $i$ is assigned a position, its connected partners $j$ can be placed by translation:
\begin{equation}
r_s(j)=r_s(i)+r_G(j)-r_G(i),\quad j\in G,
\label{eq:gp-relative}
\end{equation}
provided that all placements remain within the grid and do not conflict with occupied cells.

\par\noindent{\textbf{Policy Guided Completion.}}
For each distinct partial offspring, the policy generates the score matrix $S$ once. The Hungarian algorithm~\cite{kuhn1955hungarian} solves a one to one assignment between vacant positions $\mathcal U$ and unused fragments $\mathcal R$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal M^*=\arg\max_{\mathcal M\in\mathfrak M(\mathcal U,\mathcal R)}
\sum_{(p,i)\in\mathcal M}S_{p,i}.
\label{eq:gp-hungarian}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathfrak M(\mathcal U,\mathcal R)$ denotes all one to one complete matchings. Matched pairs are committed in descending score order. After each commitment, compatible parent shared groups are inherited. Any remaining slots are matched using the same policy scores, yielding a complete permutation.

\par\noindent{\textbf{Mutation and Refinement.}}
Nonelite offspring undergo swaps, three fragment cycles, or block exchanges. Periodic local search evaluates all two position swaps of the best candidate and accepts the highest scoring improvement. The final output is the best assembly encountered during genetic search.

\subsection{Final Reconstruction}
After determining the final piece arrangement, we reconstruct the canvas using the original, non-inpainted fragments. LaMa is then applied to the assembled canvas to fill the missing regions using global visual context, producing the final reconstructed image shown in Figure~\ref{fig:agent_comparison}. This step uses a pretrained model without additional training and does not participate in the puzzle-solving process.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp-setup}

\subsubsection{Datasets and Evaluation Metrics}
We evaluate GlobalPuzzle on GAP-3 ($3\times3$) and GAP-5 ($5\times5$)~\cite{shahar2026gap}, following the official splits of 14,000 training, 3,000 validation, and 3,000 test puzzles for each dataset. Each fragment is independently inpainted using LaMa to provide RGB inputs for the Evaluation Network. The Piece Encoder processes individual fragments, while the Policy Network uses a partial canvas and occupancy mask.

We report perfect assembly accuracy (PA) and absolute accuracy (AA), measuring the percentage of fully reconstructed puzzles and correctly placed fragments, respectively. All experiments use an NVIDIA RTX 4060 GPU, with average reconstruction times of 9 and 44 seconds per puzzle on GAP-3 and GAP-5, respectively.

\subsubsection{Network Training}
GlobalPuzzle contains three learned components: the \emph{Evaluation Network}, the \emph{Policy Network}, and the \emph{Piece Encoder}. The Piece Encoder extracts reusable fragment representations. The Policy Network combines these representations with a partial canvas to predict fragment scores for candidate positions, while the Evaluation Network predicts the quality of a complete assembled canvas.

The Evaluation Network is supervised using the quality target $Q=0.6\,\mathrm{AA}+0.4\,\mathrm{SRA}$ described in Sec.~\ref{sec:global-eval}. Training candidates include correct assemblies, permutations with controlled numbers of misplaced fragments, and structural perturbations. Its objective combines score regression, calibration, candidate ranking, hard negative supervision, and auxiliary AA prediction. We train the Evaluation Network for 50 epochs using AdamW~\cite{loshchilov2019adamw}, a batch size of 16, a learning rate of $3\times10^{-4}$, weight decay of $0.05$, and a three epoch warmup. Mixed precision training is enabled.

The Policy Network is trained on partially completed canvases containing both correct placements and deliberately perturbed placements. Supervision follows the target construction in Sec.~\ref{sec:placement-policy}: empty positions receive the corresponding ground truth fragment target when the context is correct, or a distribution derived from neighboring fragment relationships when the context is perturbed. The prediction loss is computed against all fragments, while invalid assignments are excluded when completing a puzzle. Fragment representations produced by the Piece Encoder are cached and reused during genetic search.

\subsubsection{Genetic Search Configuration}
Unless otherwise specified, we use a population of 256 individuals, 30 generations, and four elites per generation. Parents are selected using fitness weighted sampling with exponent $\beta=4$. Genetic crossover preserves shared absolute placements and shared relative fragment relationships, followed by completion using the Policy Network and assignment optimization. We use a mutation rate of $0.05$ and an additional full permutation mutation probability of $0.20$. Local refinement is performed every five generations, with at most four accepted improvement steps per invocation. To maintain consistency across comparisons, the same search budget is used for the corresponding ablation variants.

\subsubsection{Comparison Methods}
We compare GlobalPuzzle with classical optimization methods and learning based approaches. The classical group includes greedy assembly~\cite{pomeranz2011greedy} and a conventional genetic algorithm~\cite{sholomon2013genetic} based on local fragment compatibility. The learning based group includes JigsawGAN~\cite{li2022jigsawgan}, DiffAssemble~\cite{scarpellini2024diffassemble}, JPDVT~\cite{liu2024jpdvt}, FCViT~\cite{kim2025fcvit}, PuzzleFlow~\cite{shahar2026gap}, and the method of Elkin et al.~\cite{elkin2026puzlm}. These baselines cover direct arrangement prediction, learned reconstruction, and compatibility driven optimization. We additionally examine representative attempts by general purpose multimodal agents to illustrate the challenges of explicit fragment manipulation and iterative reconstruction. Since agent attempts are analyzed as qualitative case studies, they are not treated as directly comparable benchmark measurements.

\subsection{Comparison with Existing Methods and General Purpose Agents}
\label{sec:exp-comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:main_results} compares reconstruction accuracy on GAP-3 and GAP-5. GlobalPuzzle achieves PA, AA, and SRA of $69.2\%$, $80.2\%$, and $77.4\%$ on GAP-3, respectively. On the more challenging GAP-5 benchmark, the corresponding values are $19.5\%$, $48.4\%$, and $45.5\%$. These results show improvements in complete reconstruction as well as fragment placement and structural consistency relative to the baselines listed in the table. The decline from GAP-3 to GAP-5 also indicates that larger assemblies remain substantially more difficult, despite the use of global image information and iterative search.

Figure~\ref{fig:compare} compares reconstruction results from FCViT and PuzzleFlow, two leading puzzle reassembly methods, general purpose multimodal agents Codex and Claude, and our GlobalPuzzle. While existing methods and agents can recover partial image structures, they often struggle to produce fully correct arrangements. In contrast, GlobalPuzzle combines global visual evaluation with iterative genetic search to refine fragment placements and improve reconstruction accuracy.

\subsection{Ablation Studies}
\label{sec:exp-ablation}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} isolates the learned networks and genetic operations under the same datasets, metrics, and search budget. We replace each network's outputs with random scores or disable crossover, swapping, or both, while retaining the other components.

\subsubsection{Evaluation Network and Policy Network}
\noindent\textbf{Evaluation governs the search.}
The Evaluation Network has the strongest overall influence because its fitness scores direct the entire genetic algorithm: they determine elite retention, parent selection, local refinement, and the final output. Random evaluation reduces GAP-3 PA/AA from $69.2/80.2\%$ to $1.9/25.2\%$ and GAP-5 from $19.5/48.4\%$ to $0.0/9.67\%$. Even with the learned policy and genetic operators intact, uninformative fitness cannot reliably retain good candidates or favor useful changes. Candidate diversity therefore cannot compensate for losing the signal that steers evolution.

\noindent\textbf{Policy guidance becomes critical as the grid grows.}
Random policy scores retain legal assignments but remove learned placement guidance. On GAP-3, PA decreases only from $69.2\%$ to $65.1\%$, while AA falls from $80.2\%$ to $73.4\%$. A $3\times3$ puzzle has only $9!$ arrangements before constraints; inheriting fixed placements or connected groups further reduces the remaining combinations. Population diversity can thus explore many feasible completions for the trained evaluator to select from, partly compensating for a poor policy. This explains the small PA drop without implying guaranteed exhaustive enumeration. On GAP-5, random policy scores reduce PA to $0.0\%$ and AA to $16.9\%$. With $25!$ unconstrained arrangements, the same search budget cannot supply enough useful candidates through unguided completion. The policy is therefore essential for directing proposals toward promising regions of the larger search space.

\subsubsection{Genetic Search Operations}
Crossover and swapping both continually modify assemblies to supply candidates for evaluation. Crossover recombines inherited placements and connected groups, while swapping revises fragment positions within an assembly. Removing crossover leaves swapping available and yields GAP-3/GAP-5 PA of $60.0/10.4\%$; removing swapping leaves crossover available and yields $63.5/11.3\%$. Each remaining operator can still propose changes that the evaluator selects, so the framework retains partial effectiveness despite lower accuracy.

Removing both operators eliminates these complementary routes for revising candidates. GAP-5 PA/AA then collapse to $0.1/9.3\%$, compared with $19.5/48.4\%$ for the full model. GAP-3 retains $54.5/70.7\%$, consistent with the smaller search space being easier to cover. Together, these results show that evaluation chooses which candidates survive, while policy guidance and genetic variation determine which candidates become available. Reliable evaluation needs a continuing supply of useful alternatives, particularly on GAP-5.

\subsection{Real World Photo Reconstruction and Generalization}
\label{sec:exp-generalization}

To evaluate generalization beyond the GAP benchmarks, we apply GlobalPuzzle to real world photographs and puzzle configurations of different sizes, including rectangular grids such as $3\times4$, $2\times5$, and $5\times3$. All images are fragmented using the same protocol as GAP, and the pretrained networks are applied without additional training or fine tuning. Figure~\ref{fig:live} presents representative reconstruction results across different scenes and puzzle configurations.

These experiments examine the ability of GlobalPuzzle to generalize to unseen image content and grid dimensions. Position queries follow the target grid, while the Evaluation Network scores the resulting complete canvas. This allows the same learned components to support reconstruction beyond the square configurations used during training.

\section{Conclusion}
 In this paper, we proposed GlobalPuzzle, a jigsaw puzzle reassembly framework that integrates global visual evaluation, placement prediction, and genetic search. Unlike methods relying primarily on local boundary compatibility or direct permutation prediction, GlobalPuzzle evaluates complete assemblies using global visual information while supporting iterative refinement through fragment replacement and connected group relocation. By combining the Piece Encoder, Policy Network, and Evaluation Network with genetic optimization, our framework enables flexible exploration of candidate arrangements. Experiments on GAP-3 and GAP-5 demonstrate the effectiveness of this approach, particularly for challenging puzzles with irregularly eroded boundaries. Future work will investigate faster search for arbitrary fragment shapes and layouts.
