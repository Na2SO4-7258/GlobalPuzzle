\begin{abstract}
Jigsaw puzzle reassembly remains a highly challenging task, particularly when piece boundaries are irregularly eroded to simulate damage to cultural artifacts. On the GAP benchmark, existing methods achieve a perfect assembly accuracy of only 0.3\% on 5*5 puzzles, whereas humans can solve jigsaw puzzles with remarkably high accuracy. Humans typically approach puzzle solving by identifying potentially adjacent pieces, evaluating the current assembly, and iteratively performing complex operations such as adding pieces, replacing incorrectly placed pieces, and moving entire connected groups of pieces. However, existing methods face a fundamental limitation: approaches that leverage global visual information typically predict piece arrangements directly, making it difficult to explicitly perform such complex iterative operations, while search based methods can rearrange pieces but primarily rely on pairwise boundary compatibility, lacking global visual information to evaluate and guide the search process.
To bridge this gap, we propose GlobalPuzzle, a jigsaw puzzle reassembly framework that integrates global canvas level evaluation, action guidance, and genetic search. By incorporating global visual information into the evaluation and optimization of candidate assemblies, GlobalPuzzle aims to resolve ambiguous adjacency relationships that are difficult to determine from irregularly eroded piece boundaries alone, enabling more flexible and globally informed puzzle reconstruction.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Jigsaw puzzle reassembly with missing visual information is challenging due to the enormous search space and irregularly eroded piece boundaries.
An $N \times N$ puzzle has $(N^2)!$ possible arrangements, making the probability of randomly solving a $5 \times 5$ puzzle only $1/25! \approx 6.44695 \times 10^{-26}$.
Boundary erosion disrupts visual continuity, while smaller pieces provide less information for determining their positions and adjacency relationships.
Even advanced AI agents such as Claude Sonnet 5.5 Medium and Codex require approximately 10 minutes on average to solve a $3 \times 3$ GAP puzzle and rarely reconstruct $5 \times 5$ puzzles correctly.

Existing methods face two major limitations. On the one hand, many deep learning approaches exchange information among fragments through attention mechanisms and directly predict or iteratively refine their arrangements. However, such feature interaction does not explicitly construct a spatially assembled image for visual evaluation. More importantly, these methods lack explicit mechanisms for preserving established fragment connections and moving connected groups as a whole. On the other hand, combinatorial optimization methods can explore different arrangements and preserve fragment relationships through genetic operations, but typically rely on pairwise boundary compatibility to evaluate candidate solutions. When fragment boundaries are severely eroded, local compatibility scores may be insufficient to determine whether an arrangement forms a globally coherent image.
When piece boundaries are severely damaged, the arrangement with the highest local compatibility score may not form a globally semantically coherent image.

Effective puzzle reassembly requires preserving reliable fragment connections while allowing connected groups to move and reorganize as a whole. Moving fragments independently may destroy correct local structures, while permanently fixing connected groups may preserve incorrect relationships and prevent further correction. Genetic algorithms provide a natural solution to this problem. Through crossover, shared fragment connections can be inherited and relocated as complete groups, while mutation and population diversity allow incorrect structures to be broken and recombined. However, effective search also requires reliable guidance for both fragment placement and candidate evaluation. Rather than relying solely on local boundary compatibility, these decisions should incorporate global visual information from the assembled image.

Motivated by these observations, we propose GlobalPuzzle, a jigsaw puzzle reassembly framework that integrates genetic search with global visual guidance. The genetic algorithm preserves and relocates shared fragment connections through crossover, while mutation and population evolution allow these structures to be continuously revised. Two neural networks guide offspring generation and candidate evaluation. The Global Evaluation Network takes a complete assembled canvas as input and predicts a continuous quality score between 0 and 1, serving as the fitness function for genetic search. The Position Query Network takes a partially assembled canvas as input, generates query tokens for unfilled positions, and interacts with all fragment features through a Transformer to predict suitable placements. By integrating structural inheritance, position based guidance, and global canvas evaluation, GlobalPuzzle enables flexible reconstruction while guiding the search using the visual coherence of complete candidate assemblies.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Direct Arrangement Prediction Methods}

Recent learning based approaches formulate jigsaw puzzle reassembly as an arrangement prediction or position estimation problem. JigsawGAN combines permutation classification with generative adversarial learning to exploit global image semantics for arrangement prediction. FCViT employs Vision Transformers to model relationships among fragments and directly regress their two dimensional coordinates. PuzLM converts fragments into discrete visual sequences and employs a sequence generation model to predict fragment positions autoregressively. DiffAssemble and JPDVT introduce diffusion models to iteratively recover fragment arrangements through pose denoising and positional encoding denoising, respectively. PuzzleFlow further combines Vision Transformers with discrete flow matching to iteratively predict and update fragment positions based on the current arrangement.

These methods leverage attention mechanisms to exchange visual information among fragments, allowing each fragment to incorporate information from other fragments. However, such global feature interaction does not necessarily construct a spatially assembled image. Even when positional information is incorporated, the model primarily operates on fragment representations rather than explicitly evaluating the visual coherence of a complete canvas. Furthermore, although some methods iteratively adjust fragment positions, they lack explicit mechanisms for identifying, preserving, and manipulating connected fragment groups. Consequently, complex reconstruction strategies, such as preserving established connections, replacing incorrectly positioned fragments, and moving connected groups as a whole, are not explicitly supported.

\subsection{Compatibility Based Search and Optimization}

Another line of research decomposes jigsaw puzzle reassembly into fragment compatibility estimation and combinatorial optimization. Traditional methods estimate pairwise compatibility using color differences, gradient continuity, and texture information, while learning based methods employ neural networks to predict adjacency compatibility between fragments. Given these compatibility scores, greedy algorithms progressively connect fragments with high compatibility, linear programming methods jointly optimize fragment positions under global constraints, and genetic algorithms explore candidate arrangements through selection, crossover, and mutation. Some genetic algorithms further preserve existing fragment connections during crossover, allowing connected groups to be rearranged as a whole.

Unlike direct prediction methods, search based approaches can explicitly explore different arrangements and perform more flexible structural operations. However, their objective functions primarily rely on local pairwise compatibility rather than the visual coherence of the complete assembled image. When fragment boundaries are severely eroded, local compatibility information may become insufficient to reliably distinguish candidate arrangements. Therefore, integrating global visual evaluation of complete canvases with explicit structural operations provides a promising direction for guiding the search and optimization of jigsaw puzzle reconstruction.

Although these methods can explore different puzzle configurations through combinatorial optimization, their objective functions are primarily based on local pairwise compatibility rather than the visual coherence of the complete assembled image. In contrast, GlobalPuzzle integrates learned global canvas evaluation with genetic search. It generates candidate arrangements through position based queries, preserves piece relationships through structural inheritance, and evaluates each assembled canvas using a visual fitness network. The resulting global fitness scores guide the search and optimization process.


\section{Method}

\subsection{Framework Overview}
\label{sec:overview}

Given $M=N^2$ unordered image fragments $\mathcal{P}=\{I_i\}_{i=1}^{M}$, we seek a permutation $\pi$ assigning one fragment to each position of an $N\times N$ grid. Let $C(\pi)$ denote the resulting RGB canvas. GlobalPuzzle searches for the assembly maximizing a learned quality function:
\begin{equation}
    \hat\pi=\arg\max_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_M}F_\theta(\pi),\qquad
    F_\theta(\pi)=\sigma\bigl(A_\theta(C(\pi))\bigr).
    \label{eq:gp-objective}
\end{equation}
The framework combines a global evaluation network $A_\theta$, a canvas conditioned placement policy $B_\phi$, and genetic search. The policy proposes placements, genetic operations preserve and rearrange fragment groups, and the evaluator selects promising complete assemblies.

Before assembly, we independently apply LaMa to each eroded puzzle piece without access to the ground-truth arrangement or complete image. This provides dense RGB inputs for subsequent processing but does not guarantee pixel or boundary continuity between adjacent pieces.
\subsection{Evaluation Network}
\label{sec:global-eval}

    \centering

       The specific structure of the Evaluation Network and Policy Network

\textbf{Architecture.}
The evaluator processes the resulting complete canvas using a multiscale CNN followed by a Transformer. Features pooled to the puzzle grid retain spatial organization, while a global token and spatial readout provide complementary representations. The network outputs a quality logit $z_\theta=A_\theta(C(\pi))$ and a fitness score $\hat Q(\pi)=\sigma(z_\theta)$. An auxiliary head estimates absolute accuracy during training.
The evaluator processes a complete canvas using a multiscale CNN followed by a Transformer. Features pooled to the puzzle grid retain spatial organization, while a global token and spatial readout provide complementary representations. The network outputs a quality logit $z_\theta=A_\theta(C(\pi))$ and a fitness score $\hat Q(\pi)=\sigma(z_\theta)$. An auxiliary head estimates absolute accuracy during training.
\noindent
\textbf{Quality supervision.}
Let $\pi^*$ be the ground truth, $r_\pi(i)$ the grid coordinate of fragment $i$, and $\mathcal E^*$ the set of $2N(N-1)$ oriented ground truth adjacencies $(i,j,\delta)$. We define
\begin{align}
\mathrm{AA}(\pi)&=\frac1M\sum_{p=1}^{M}\mathbf1[\pi(p)=\pi^*(p)],\label{eq:gp-aa}\\
\mathrm{SRA}(\pi)&=\frac1{|\mathcal E^*|}\sum_{(i,j,\delta)\in\mathcal E^*}
\mathbf1[r_\pi(j)-r_\pi(i)=\delta],\label{eq:gp-sra}\\
Q(\pi)&=0.6\,\mathrm{AA}(\pi)+0.4\,\mathrm{SRA}(\pi).
\label{eq:gp-quality}
\end{align}
We generate training assemblies with controlled displacement counts and structural transformations, including block exchanges, row and column shifts, and mixed mutations.
\noindent
\textbf{Training objective.}
The evaluator is trained with logit regression, score calibration, within puzzle ranking, hard negative mining, and auxiliary AA regression:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_A=\mathcal L_{\rm reg}+0.5\mathcal L_{\rm cal}
+0.75\mathcal L_{\rm rank}+0.5\mathcal L_{\rm hard}
+0.2\mathcal L_{\rm AA}.
\label{eq:gp-a-loss}
\end{equation}
Regression targets are $\operatorname{logit}(\operatorname{clip}(Q,0.01,0.99))$. For candidates $i,j$ of the same puzzle with $Q_i>Q_j$, the ranking term penalizes
\begin{equation}
\ell_{ij}=\operatorname{softplus}\bigl(3(Q_i-Q_j)-z_i+z_j\bigr),
\label{eq:gp-rank}
\end{equation}
with larger weights for greater quality differences and structural negatives. The hard negative term penalizes the most overestimated incorrect candidate relative to the correct assembly.

\subsection{Policy Network}
\label{sec:placement-policy}

The policy encodes all fragments into tokens $c_i$ and extracts a query $q_p$ for each grid position from the partial RGB canvas and its occupancy map. A joint Transformer updates both token sets. The resulting pairwise scores are
\begin{equation}
S_{p,i}=\tau\,
\frac{\langle W_q\tilde q_p,W_c\tilde c_i\rangle}
{\|W_q\tilde q_p\|_2\,\|W_c\tilde c_i\|_2},
\label{eq:gp-policy}
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is learned. One forward pass produces an $M\times M$ position to fragment score matrix.

Training uses correct and perturbed partial canvases. When all occupied fragments are correctly placed, empty positions receive one hot ground truth targets. Otherwise, occupied neighbors vote for fragments that preserve their ground truth directional relationships. Normalized vote counts define targets $t_{p,i}$, and the policy minimizes
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_B=-\frac1{|\mathcal Q|}\sum_{p\in\mathcal Q}\sum_{i=1}^{M}
 t_{p,i}\log\operatorname{softmax}(S_{p,:})_i,
\label{eq:gp-b-loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal Q$ contains supervised empty positions. During search, occupied positions and used fragments are masked. Fragment embeddings are cached for reuse across candidates.

\subsection{Genetic Search with Global Fitness}
\label{sec:ga}
\noindent
\textbf{Initialization and selection.}
The policy generates an initial population of complete permutations. Each is evaluated by $F_\theta$. The strongest individuals are retained as elites, while parents are sampled with fitness weighted probabilities
\begin{equation}
P(\pi_k)=\frac{(F_\theta(\pi_k)+\epsilon)^\beta}
{\sum_j(F_\theta(\pi_j)+\epsilon)^\beta}.
\label{eq:gp-selection}
\end{equation}
\noindent
\textbf{Structural crossover.}
Given parents $\pi^a$ and $\pi^b$, identical absolute placements are copied into the offspring:
\begin{equation}
s_p=\pi^a(p)\quad\text{if }\pi^a(p)=\pi^b(p).
\label{eq:gp-absolute}
\end{equation}
We then identify oriented adjacencies shared by both parents. Their connected components form relative fragment groups. When a fragment $i$ is assigned a position, its connected partners $j$ can be placed by translation:
\begin{equation}
r_s(j)=r_s(i)+r_G(j)-r_G(i),\quad j\in G,
\label{eq:gp-relative}
\end{equation}
provided that all placements remain within the grid and do not conflict with occupied cells.

\noindent
\textbf{Policy guided completion.}
For each distinct partial offspring, the policy generates the score matrix $S$ once. The Hungarian algorithm solves a one to one assignment between vacant positions $\mathcal U$ and unused fragments $\mathcal R$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal M^*=\arg\max_{\mathcal M\in\mathfrak M(\mathcal U,\mathcal R)}
\sum_{(p,i)\in\mathcal M}S_{p,i}.
\label{eq:gp-hungarian}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathfrak M(\mathcal U,\mathcal R)$ denotes all one to one complete matchings. Matched pairs are committed in descending score order. After each commitment, compatible parent shared groups are inherited. Any remaining slots are matched using the same policy scores, yielding a complete permutation.

\noindent
\textbf{Mutation and refinement.}
Nonelite offspring undergo swaps, three fragment cycles, or block exchanges. Periodic local search evaluates all two position swaps of the best candidate and accepts the highest scoring improvement. The final output is the best assembly encountered during genetic search.

\subsection{Final Reconstruction}
After determining the final piece arrangement, we reconstruct the canvas using the original, non-inpainted fragments. LaMa is then applied to the assembled canvas to fill the missing regions using global visual context, producing the final reconstructed image shown in Figure 1. This step uses a pretrained model without additional training and does not participate in the puzzle-solving process.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp-setup}

\subsubsection{Datasets and Evaluation Metrics}
We evaluate GlobalPuzzle on GAP-3 and GAP-5, which contain irregularly eroded image fragments arranged on $3\times3$ and $5\times5$ grids, respectively. We follow the dataset splits for training and evaluation. For complete canvas evaluation, each fragment is independently processed by LaMa to fill its missing regions. This operation supplies dense RGB inputs to the Evaluator Network without explicitly enforcing continuity between neighboring fragments. The Piece Encoder uses fragment information, while the Policy Network conditions placement queries on a partial canvas and its occupancy mask.

We report three standard metrics: perfect assembly accuracy (PA), absolute accuracy (AA), and structure reassembly accuracy (SRA). PA is the percentage of puzzles for which every fragment is placed correctly. AA measures the fraction of fragments assigned to their correct absolute positions, while SRA measures the proportion of ground truth oriented adjacency relationships preserved in the predicted arrangement. All three metrics are reported as percentages, with higher values indicating better reconstruction. PA measures complete puzzle recovery, whereas AA and SRA provide complementary information about partial correctness and structural consistency.

\subsubsection{Network Training}
GlobalPuzzle contains three learned components: the \emph{Evaluator Network}, the \emph{Policy Network}, and the \emph{Piece Encoder}. The Piece Encoder extracts reusable fragment representations. The Policy Network combines these representations with a partial canvas to predict fragment scores for candidate positions, while the Evaluator Network predicts the quality of a complete assembled canvas.

The Evaluator Network is supervised using the quality target $Q=0.6\,\mathrm{AA}+0.4\,\mathrm{SRA}$ described in Sec.~\ref{sec:global-eval}. Training candidates include correct assemblies, permutations with controlled numbers of misplaced fragments, and structural perturbations. Its objective combines score regression, calibration, candidate ranking, hard negative supervision, and auxiliary AA prediction. We train the Evaluator Network for 50 epochs using AdamW, a batch size of 16, a learning rate of $3\times10^{-4}$, weight decay of $0.05$, and a three epoch warmup. Mixed precision training is enabled.

The Policy Network is trained on partially completed canvases containing both correct placements and deliberately perturbed placements. Supervision follows the target construction in Sec.~\ref{sec:placement-policy}: empty positions receive the corresponding ground truth fragment target when the context is correct, or a distribution derived from neighboring fragment relationships when the context is perturbed. The prediction loss is computed against all fragments, while invalid assignments are excluded when completing a puzzle. Fragment representations produced by the Piece Encoder are cached and reused during genetic search.

\subsubsection{Genetic Search Configuration}
Unless otherwise specified, we use a population of 256 individuals, 30 generations, and four elites per generation. Parents are selected using fitness weighted sampling with exponent $\beta=4$. Genetic crossover preserves shared absolute placements and shared relative fragment relationships, followed by completion using the Policy Network and assignment optimization. We use a mutation rate of $0.05$ and an additional full permutation mutation probability of $0.20$. Local refinement is performed every five generations, with at most four accepted improvement steps per invocation. To maintain consistency across comparisons, the same search budget is used for the corresponding ablation variants.

\subsubsection{Comparison Methods}
We compare GlobalPuzzle with classical optimization methods and learning based approaches. The classical group includes greedy assembly and a conventional genetic algorithm based on local fragment compatibility. The learning based group includes JigsawGAN, DiffAssemble, JPDVT, FCViT, PuzzleFlow, and the method of Elkin et al. These baselines cover direct arrangement prediction, learned reconstruction, and compatibility driven optimization. We additionally examine representative attempts by general purpose multimodal agents to illustrate the challenges of explicit fragment manipulation and iterative reconstruction. Since agent attempts are analyzed as qualitative case studies, they are not treated as directly comparable benchmark measurements.

\subsection{Comparison with Existing Methods and General Purpose Agents}
\label{sec:exp-comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:main_results} compares reconstruction accuracy on GAP-3 and GAP-5. In the current experimental results, GlobalPuzzle achieves PA, AA, and SRA of $69.2\%$, $80.2\%$, and $77.4\%$ on GAP-3, respectively. On the more challenging GAP-5 benchmark, the corresponding values are $19.5\%$, $48.4\%$, and $45.5\%$. These results show improvements in complete reconstruction as well as fragment placement and structural consistency relative to the baselines listed in the table. The decline from GAP-3 to GAP-5 also indicates that larger assemblies remain substantially more difficult, despite the use of global image information and iterative search.

The qualitative comparison in Fig.~\ref{fig:method-comparison} examines how different approaches handle missing boundaries and ambiguous visual content. Methods relying primarily on local compatibility may preserve some plausible neighboring relationships while placing larger image regions incorrectly. Direct prediction methods can recover substantial visual structure but do not explicitly revise a completed arrangement through subsequent search operations. GlobalPuzzle instead evaluates each candidate as a complete image and can revise fragment assignments, including the placement of connected groups, across generations. These capabilities are especially relevant when a locally plausible arrangement conflicts with the broader scene.

We also examine reconstruction attempts by general purpose multimodal agents, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:method-comparison}. Such agents can often identify semantic cues and propose plausible fragment relationships, but obtaining an exact permutation requires consistently tracking all fragment identities, positions, and adjacency constraints. Representative failures illustrate the gap between visually reasoning about the scene and reliably optimizing a complete combinatorial arrangement. These examples are intended to characterize common difficulties rather than establish a quantitative ranking against specialized puzzle solvers.

\subsection{Ablation Studies}
\label{sec:exp-ablation}

We conduct controlled ablations to identify the contributions of the learned networks and the main genetic search operations. The variants are evaluated using the same datasets, metrics, and search budget as the full framework. Results are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:ablation}.

\subsubsection{Evaluator Network and Policy Network}
To assess the Evaluator Network, we replace its predicted fitness with the ground truth AA, ground truth SRA, or random fitness while keeping the remaining search procedure unchanged. Oracle AA and SRA provide diagnostic upper reference conditions for different search objectives, whereas random fitness isolates the effect of losing informative candidate evaluation. Importantly, an oracle objective does not guarantee that a finite search will reach its optimum.

To assess the Policy Network, we replace its predicted fragment scores with either an oracle placement policy or random scores, retaining the same legal assignment constraints. This comparison evaluates whether learned position queries improve offspring completion beyond stochastic assignment, and how much performance remains limited by placement decisions when evaluation and genetic operators are unchanged.

\subsubsection{Genetic Search Operations}
We separately remove shared absolute placement inheritance and shared relative adjacency inheritance to examine their contributions to offspring construction. The former transfers positions that both parents agree upon, while the latter preserves fragment groups whose relative geometry is consistent across parents and allows these groups to be translated when feasible. We further disable mutation and local refinement individually to evaluate their effects on search diversity and solution quality. These ablations distinguish the benefits of inheriting existing structures from the ability to escape incorrect structures through subsequent modifications.

\subsection{Analysis of the Evaluator Network and Policy Network}
\label{sec:exp-network-analysis}

\subsubsection{Evaluator Network}
We assess whether the Evaluator Network provides a reliable optimization signal beyond merely distinguishing complete solutions from obviously incorrect ones. For candidate assemblies with known ground truth, we compare predicted fitness against the target quality $Q$ and measure their correlation. Figure~\ref{fig:fitness-scatter} visualizes the correspondence between predicted and true quality across different levels of reconstruction accuracy. This analysis reveals both the calibration of the predicted scores and any systematic deviations for low quality or nearly correct assemblies.

We additionally examine ranking accuracy by sampling pairs of candidate assemblies from the same puzzle and measuring how frequently the predicted fitness preserves their true quality ordering. Candidates are grouped by the number of misplaced fragments and by perturbation type. In particular, block swaps, row and column shifts, and other structured rearrangements test whether the evaluator detects coherent but globally misplaced image regions. Unlike a measure based only on local boundary agreement, this evaluation probes the network's sensitivity to the overall visual organization of the assembled image.

\subsubsection{Policy Network}
We measure the Policy Network using top one fragment prediction accuracy over supervised empty positions. A prediction is correct when the highest scoring fragment matches the target, with accuracy computed from the prediction scores before the assignment stage. This isolates the learned placement signal from the effects of the Hungarian algorithm and later genetic search.

We report results separately for GAP-3 and GAP-5 to study the effect of increasing the number of candidate fragments. We also distinguish partial canvases containing only correctly placed fragments from those containing incorrect placements. The latter setting is important because candidate assemblies encountered during genetic search are not necessarily consistent with the ground truth. Finally, we group queries by the number of occupied positions to examine how the available visual context affects prediction accuracy. These analyses characterize both the strengths and limitations of the Policy Network under the varying canvas states encountered during reconstruction.

\subsection{Analysis of the Genetic Search Process}
\label{sec:exp-search-analysis}

We analyze the evolution of candidate assemblies throughout genetic search. Figure~\ref{fig:search-evolution} presents the best candidate from selected generations together with its reconstruction quality. This visualization illustrates how search can improve a partially correct arrangement through changes in individual fragment positions and the translation of connected fragment groups. Such group operations are particularly useful when several fragments have a consistent internal arrangement but occupy an incorrect region of the canvas.

We further examine the effect of the number of generations on final PA, AA, and SRA. Alongside these reconstruction metrics, we record the best predicted fitness and the diversity of candidate arrangements throughout search. Because elites are preserved, the best predicted fitness cannot decrease between generations. Ground truth reconstruction accuracy, however, need not improve monotonically, since the Evaluator Network is an approximation of the target quality. Comparing these curves helps distinguish limited search exploration from errors in the learned evaluation signal.

\subsection{Real World Photo Reconstruction and Generalization}
\label{sec:exp-generalization}

To examine generalization beyond the artwork dominated benchmark images, we additionally consider photographs captured in everyday environments. The photographs cover different scene types, textures, and visual structures and are converted into irregular fragment puzzles using the same fragmentation protocol as the benchmark. We apply the trained GlobalPuzzle framework without adapting the networks to individual test images. Figure~\ref{fig:real-world} presents representative reconstructions at both $3\times3$ and $5\times5$ resolutions.

This evaluation examines whether the learned global quality signal and placement policy remain useful when the image content differs from that seen during training. Photographs containing distinctive objects and coherent large scale geometry may provide useful cues for global evaluation, whereas repeated textures, uniform backgrounds, and weak visual landmarks can create additional ambiguity. Comparing the two puzzle sizes allows us to analyze how these factors interact with the larger combinatorial search space. The results provide a complementary assessment of transferability beyond the GAP benchmarks.

\subsection{Computational Efficiency}
\label{sec:exp-efficiency}

We analyze the computational requirements of GlobalPuzzle in terms of model size and reconstruction time. Parameter counts are reported separately for the Piece Encoder, the Policy Network excluding the Piece Encoder, and the Evaluator Network, together with the total number of unique trainable parameters. For inference efficiency, the Piece Encoder computes reusable fragment features, and repeated candidate evaluations are performed in batches whenever possible.

We report the mean solving time per puzzle on GAP-3 and GAP-5 under identical hardware and search settings. We additionally vary population size and the number of generations to study the tradeoff between computation and reconstruction accuracy. Larger search budgets permit more candidate evaluations and structural rearrangements but increase inference cost. This analysis clarifies how the framework scales from nine to twenty five fragments and identifies practical operating points for accuracy and runtime.


\section{Conclusion}
 In this paper, we proposed GlobalPuzzle, a jigsaw puzzle reassembly framework that integrates global visual evaluation, placement prediction, and genetic search. Unlike methods relying primarily on local boundary compatibility or direct permutation prediction, GlobalPuzzle evaluates complete assemblies using global visual information while supporting iterative refinement through fragment replacement and connected group relocation. By combining the Piece Encoder, Policy Network, and Evaluator Network with genetic optimization, our framework enables flexible exploration of candidate arrangements. Experiments on GAP-3 and GAP-5 demonstrate the effectiveness of this approach, particularly for challenging puzzles with irregularly eroded boundaries. Future work will investigate more efficient search strategies and extend the framework to puzzles with arbitrary fragment shapes and layouts.
